    Frequently Asked Questions

    How do I know whether my soil needs lime?

    Lime need varies by soil and crop. A laboratory assessment of soil acidity and buffering is needed to recommend a rate. A pH reading is a screening measurement, not an application prescription.

    Can I use wood ash instead of agricultural lime?

    Wood ash may raise pH, but composition and neutralizing value vary. Have the soil and ash assessed before use; there is no universal substitution rate.

    What is dolomitic lime and when should I use it?

    Dolomitic limestone can supply magnesium as well as neutralize acidity. Choose it when the soil recommendation supports that material; the product name alone does not establish its effectiveness.

    My soil is alkaline — what do I use?

    High pH alone does not establish sodicity or an amendment dose. Ask a soil laboratory to assess salinity, exchangeable sodium or SAR, carbonate content and amendment requirements. Gypsum may be appropriate for diagnosed sodic soil but does not acidify soil. Sulphur suitability and rate depend on soil buffering and carbonate content; lowering pH in calcareous soil may be impractical.

    How long does liming take to work?

    Reaction time depends on material fineness, incorporation, moisture, temperature and soil conditions. Follow a locally appropriate application and retesting plan.

    Should I test my soil before liming?

    Obtain a laboratory recommendation before selecting an application rate. Ask the laboratory about sampling, crop requirements, treatment depth, product specifications and local testing costs.

    Soil pH and African Agriculture

    Soil pH is a measure of acidity or alkalinity on a scale of 0–14, with 7 being neutral. Most African soils range from pH 4.5 to 8.5. In the humid tropics — across West, Central, and East Africa — soils tend to be acidic because heavy rainfall leaches basic cations (calcium, magnesium, potassium) out of the root zone, leaving behind hydrogen and aluminium ions. In the semi-arid Sahel, Egypt, Sudan, and parts of Southern Africa, low rainfall and calcareous parent material produce alkaline soils.

    Why pH Matters for Crop Yields

    Soil pH controls the availability of nearly all plant nutrients. At low pH (< 5.5), phosphorus binds to aluminium and iron and becomes unavailable to plants, while aluminium and manganese reach toxic levels. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legume root nodules also struggle below pH 5.8. At high pH (> 7.5), micronutrients like zinc, iron, and manganese become insoluble. The "ideal" pH range of 5.5–7.0 maximises the availability of most nutrients simultaneously — which is why lime application is one of the highest-return investments in African smallholder farming.

    Wood Ash Requires Material Testing

    Wood ash composition varies. Its suitability depends on the soil test and analysis of the actual material, including neutralizing value, nutrient content and contaminants. This tool does not calculate an ash rate from pH.
