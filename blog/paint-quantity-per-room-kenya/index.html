Paint quantity for a room in Kenya depends on measured surface area, number of coats, product spreading rate, surface porosity, colour change and preparation. Room count alone is unreliable because ceiling height, windows and product systems differ.

A material or power estimate is only as reliable as its measurements, product specification and safety assumptions. Area alone is rarely enough: thickness, overlap, openings, wastage, starting surge and usable capacity can change the purchase.

Quick answer

Measure wall perimeter and height, subtract large doors and windows, add the ceiling if it will be painted, and keep primer, undercoat and finish coats separate. Multiply net area by coats and divide by the selected product's spreading rate per coat. Round to available pack sizes only after adding an explained allowance for texture, porosity and application loss.

Why this calculation or checklist matters

Keep a measurement sheet beside the estimate. Label every dimension and unit, identify the chosen product, record the manufacturer coverage or effective width, and separate measured quantity from contingency. Structural and electrical choices still require a competent professional.

A label coverage figure often assumes a prepared surface and stated film thickness. Rough plaster, repairs, strong colour changes and absorbent surfaces can require preparation or additional coats. Buying by a generic average can leave mismatched batches or unnecessary leftovers.

A good working file also makes disagreement cheaper. Instead of arguing about a total, the people involved can inspect the quantity, unit, date, source and assumption that produced it. That is the difference between a reusable estimate and a number copied into a message.

Step-by-step workflow

  1. Step 1. Measure each wall and ceiling surface in metres and calculate square metres.
  2. Step 2. Subtract only openings large enough to affect procurement and record them separately.
  3. Step 3. Inspect the substrate and follow the selected product's preparation guidance.
  4. Step 4. Enter the product coverage per coat and the required number of coats for each layer.
  5. Step 5. Convert litres to available pack sizes and keep colours and finishes separate.
  6. Step 6. Record actual use and unopened leftovers so the next room estimate improves.

Calculate net paintable area

For rectangular walls, multiply each wall length by height and sum the results. Subtract measured doors, windows, tiles or built-ins that will not be painted. Add the ceiling separately.

Do not mix square feet and square metres. Keep a sketch with labels because room shapes, stairwells and high walls are easy to omit.

  • Opening area
  • Net wall area
  • Ceiling and trim area

Use the exact product coverage

Coverage varies by formulation and surface. Use the manufacturer data for the exact primer or finish, and note whether the figure is per coat.

A general Crown Paints planning guide gives a useful starting context, but the selected product data and substrate condition should control the final estimate.

  • Product and colour
  • Spreading rate per coat
  • Number of coats
  • Surface preparation requirement

Round into a purchase plan

Calculate primer and each finish colour separately. Round to combinations of pack sizes that reduce leftover paint without risking a mid-job shortage.

Keep batch information for large continuous walls and store any usable leftover safely according to product guidance. Do not pour paint or washings into drains.

Build an evidence pack another person can audit

Walk the site with the drawing, product data and estimate. Check each dimension at the point it is used and photograph labels or conditions that affect coverage, thickness, overlap, rating or safety. Record who measured, the instrument, date and unit. If the drawing and site disagree, stop the quantity decision and obtain a resolved instruction instead of averaging the two numbers.

Use short filenames that begin with the date and describe the record. Keep the original source separate from calculations and annotations. Where a file contains identity, financial, health or commercial data, share the minimum necessary information and use the official or trusted channel. A checklist should reduce exposure, not create another uncontrolled copy of sensitive material.

Set the decision gate

Ask the responsible engineer, electrician, quantity surveyor, installer or manufacturer to confirm the safety-critical specification and installation assumptions. The commercial order should name the exact product, size, rating, finish and accessory scope. Keep substitutions pending until their dimensions and performance have been recalculated and approved.

Write a clear stop condition before commitment. A stop condition might be a missing official search, an unverified payment account, an unresolved name mismatch, an unaffordable pressure case, an unapproved safety specification or a supplier quote that excludes essential scope. When it appears, pause and resolve the evidence rather than pushing the same uncertain assumption into the final output. Record who made the final decision, the date and the evidence they reviewed so a later update has an honest starting point.

Stress-test the result

Calculate a measured case, a procurement case and a contingency case. The measured case shows the theoretical quantity. The procurement case rounds to the packs, sheets or equipment sizes actually sold. The contingency case adds only an explained allowance for cutting, breakage, rework, surge or future load.

Lower the spreading rate for a rough or absorbent wall and add one extra finish coat for a major colour change. If the purchase changes substantially, test a small area and confirm preparation with the manufacturer or painter before buying everything.

Write the decision beside the scenario. Examples include delaying a purchase, collecting a larger deposit, reducing scope, changing packaging, adding route density, choosing a different loan, or asking a qualified adviser to verify an exception. A scenario without a decision is only another spreadsheet column.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Estimating from room count without measuring wall height.
  • Forgetting that coverage is usually stated per coat.
  • Using one coverage value for primer and finish paint.
  • Subtracting every small opening while ignoring texture and porosity.
  • Mixing colours or finishes in one grand litre total.

One final check catches many errors: ask whether a different person could reproduce the answer from the saved inputs. If not, label the missing assumption before using the result in a quote, purchase, application or public promise.
