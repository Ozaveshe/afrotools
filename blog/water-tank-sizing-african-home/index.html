Water Tank Sizing for an African Home

The right household water tank is not simply the largest tank that fits the budget. It must cover realistic daily demand through the intended interruption period, fit the site, work with the pump and plumbing arrangement, and preserve water quality. Oversizing can create stagnant storage and structural cost; undersizing creates repeated shortages.

Decision rule: size usable storage from measured essential demand and the credible refill gap, then have the site, support, plumbing and water-safety arrangement checked before purchase.

Start with the purpose of storage

Define whether the tank is for short municipal interruptions, tanker delivery, borehole pumping windows, rainwater harvesting, emergency reserve, irrigation or a combination. Drinking-water storage has different hygiene requirements from landscape or construction water.

Write down the service level the household wants: essential use only, normal use, or normal use plus outdoor demand. This keeps a comfort assumption from being mistaken for a health minimum.

The basic sizing equation

A practical starting point is: required usable capacity = household daily demand × backup days. Add a measured buffer for uncertainty, then select the next appropriate standard tank size. For a property supplied every three days, the model should cover the longest credible interval rather than the advertised schedule alone.

Usable capacity may be lower than the nominal label because outlets, sediment space, overflow level and operating controls leave some volume unavailable. Confirm manufacturer dimensions and usable volume before purchase.

Estimate daily demand by activity

List drinking and cooking, handwashing, bathing, toilets, laundry, cleaning, pets, gardens and any home business. Use meter readings, tanker volumes or tank drawdown where possible. Actual household evidence is stronger than a universal per-person figure.

WHO guidance connects domestic water quantity, accessibility and health, but a public-health service target is not a building-design instruction for every property. Local climate, fixtures, occupancy, accessibility and supply reliability matter.

Separate essential and discretionary demand

Create an essential-use plan for outages and a normal-use plan for ordinary operation. During a shortage, garden irrigation, vehicle washing and some cleaning can be reduced while drinking, food preparation and hygiene remain protected.

For mixed residential and commercial premises, calculate the shop, salon, clinic, kitchen, guest room or rental unit separately. A household-only assumption can materially understate demand.

Choose the backup period

Use actual interruption records. Consider the interval between municipal supply windows, realistic tanker availability, borehole downtime, pump repair time and seasonal access. A longer backup period increases resilience but also increases weight, cost and water age.

Where supply is extremely irregular, consider multiple tanks or a staged system. Separating drinking-water reserve from general-use storage can make hygiene and rationing easier.

Check structure, location and access

One litre of water has a mass of about one kilogram, before the tank and support structure are added. Elevated and rooftop installations require qualified structural assessment. The base must be level, stable and designed for the filled load.

Check delivery access, maintenance clearance, sun exposure, overflow route, drainage, pump noise and future replacement. A cheap location can become expensive if the tank cannot be cleaned or removed safely.

Plan plumbing and pressure

A storage tank does not automatically provide adequate pressure. Gravity pressure depends on elevation; pumped systems depend on pump curve, pipe size, controls and peak demand. Protect the pump against dry running and design overflow where it will not damage foundations or neighbours.

Use isolation valves, screened vents, secure lids and accessible drain or cleaning points. Cross-connections between potable and non-potable systems require professional attention.

Protect stored-water quality

Keep the tank covered, screened against insects and protected from contamination. Clean and inspect it on an appropriate local schedule. Rainwater systems need roof, gutter, first-flush and treatment decisions suited to intended use.

Do not assume clear water is safe to drink. Follow local public-health guidance for treatment and testing, especially after contamination, long storage, flooding or repairs.

Common sizing mistakes

  • Using a generic litres-per-person figure without checking actual use.
  • Ignoring guests, tenants, home businesses, gardens or livestock.
  • Using nominal tank volume as fully usable volume.
  • Choosing backup days from a normal week rather than credible outages.
  • Placing a full tank on an unverified roof or weak platform.
  • Buying storage without planning pumps, overflow, cleaning and water quality.

Source and professional boundary

WHO, Domestic water quantity, service level and health. This guide is a planning framework, not plumbing, structural-engineering or public-health approval. Confirm local building requirements, water authority rules, manufacturer instructions and site design with qualified professionals.

How to validate the planning model

Multiply the household’s measured essential daily demand by its chosen backup interval, then compare the result with usable rather than nominal storage. If the plumbing arrangement leaves part of the tank unavailable or refill timing is uncertain, record that constraint before selecting a standard capacity.

Repeat the model with normal-use and essential-use demand. The difference shows how long the same tank can last under an agreed outage plan and makes the conservation assumption visible.

Rainwater harvesting needs a water balance

Roof area and rainfall determine potential collection, while losses occur through first flush, splash, leakage and system efficiency. Tank size alone does not guarantee supply through a dry period. Use local rainfall records, realistic catchment area, household demand and overflow frequency.

Separate rainwater by intended use and treatment. Roof material, nearby trees, birds, dust, air pollution and maintenance affect quality. Follow local health guidance before using harvested water for drinking or food preparation.

Operations matter after installation

Create a simple operating plan: inspect screens and lids, test float and pump controls, check overflow, remove sediment and document cleaning. Monitor unexpected drawdown because hidden leaks can empty a correctly sized tank. After maintenance, protect the system from recontamination.

Where tenants or several households share storage, agree who monitors level, orders refills, pays energy and tanker costs, and reports leaks. Shared infrastructure fails when responsibility is undefined.

Questions to ask before buying

  • What demand was actually measured, and over what period?
  • Which uses must continue during an outage?
  • What is the longest credible refill interval?
  • What volume is truly usable?
  • Can the site carry the full weight safely?
  • How will water be treated, inspected and cleaned?
  • How will pressure, overflow and pump protection work?

A seven-day demand audit

Before selecting capacity, read the meter or record tank level at the same time each day for one representative week. Note occupancy, laundry, cleaning, guests, garden use and supply interruptions. Separate unusual events from routine demand. Where metering is unavailable, record refill volumes and the number of days each refill lasts.

Repeat the exercise in a dry or high-demand period if seasonality is material. The objective is not false precision. It is a household-specific starting point that can be explained to the plumber, structural professional or supplier.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate water tank size for a family?

Estimate verified daily demand, multiply by the intended backup days, allow for unusable volume and uncertainty, then choose a suitable standard size.

Is a bigger water tank always better?

No. Bigger tanks cost more, weigh more and may increase water age. Size for a defined service need and safe site.

Can I put a water tank on my roof?

Only after a qualified assessment of the filled load, supports, anchoring, access and plumbing.

Can stored tank water be used for drinking?

Only when the source, storage, treatment and testing meet applicable public-health requirements.
