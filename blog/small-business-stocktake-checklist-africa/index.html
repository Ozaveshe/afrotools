Small Business Stocktake Checklist for African Shops

AfroTools TeamSeptember 7, 202612 min read

A stocktake is the controlled process of checking what a business physically holds against what its records say it holds. It is more than walking through a shop and estimating what looks low. A useful count identifies each item consistently, fixes a cut-off time, records condition and location, explains movements during the count, and preserves differences for review.

Use the AfroTools Inventory Calculator and Local Stock Tracker to keep a private product list with SKU, category, unit cost, selling price, quantity on hand, reorder point and optional target stock. The tool works in the browser and can export CSV, JSON and PDF summaries. It does not see shelves, import invoices, tax systems, supplier deliveries or sales terminals, so the physical count and evidence still come from the business.

Sources reviewed: September 7, 2026. This checklist uses current public guidance from the Uganda Revenue Authority, Kenya Revenue Authority and Ghana Revenue Authority, plus the IFRS Foundation overview of IAS 2. Country rules and tax systems differ. Confirm the requirements that apply to your registration, sector and location before using a stock record for filing or formal accounts.

Count first, explain second, adjust third. Preserve the original book quantity and the first physical count. A variance is a question to investigate, not permission to change a number until the records agree.

1. Decide what this stocktake must answer

Define the location, date, cut-off time and stock categories in scope. A single shop may hold goods on shelves, behind a counter, in a storeroom, with a delivery rider, at a second branch, on consignment or in a returns area. State whether each location is included. Do not add expected deliveries or customer returns that have not physically arrived by the cut-off.

Choose the decisions that will follow. The count may support a routine weekly check, a month-end reconciliation, an annual accounting close, a transfer between staff, an insurance record or a purchase plan. Those uses need different evidence. A quick cycle count of selected fast-moving items can strengthen daily control, but it does not prove that every item was counted.

Assign roles before the count begins. One person can count while another records or verifies higher-risk lines. If the same person receives, stores, sells, counts and approves every adjustment, errors are harder to detect. Small teams may not be able to separate every duty, but they can require a second signature, owner review or photographed shelf label for material adjustments.

2. Build a clean item list before touching the shelves

Use one line for each product variant that must be controlled separately. Size, flavour, colour, model, strength, pack type and condition can change price, expiry or reorder decisions. Give each line a stable name and SKU where possible. Do not use one name for several items merely because customers describe them together.

Define the unit of count. A carton, inner pack, bottle, metre, kilogram and individual piece are different units. If the business buys cartons but sells pieces, record the verified conversion and decide whether the stock record will use cartons, pieces or both in separate fields. The unit must remain consistent between opening quantity, purchases, sales, adjustments and physical count.

Print or export a count sheet that does not invite counters to copy the expected quantity. A blind count can reduce the temptation to make the shelf number match the book number. Keep the book quantity available to the reviewer for later reconciliation.

3. Set a cut-off and control every movement

The cleanest method is to pause receiving, selling, returns and transfers during the count. Lock completed areas against new movement and mark them visibly. If trading cannot stop, use a movement log with exact time, item, unit, quantity, direction and reference. The reviewer must place each movement on the correct side of the cut-off.

Complete pending paperwork before counting. Match goods already received to delivery notes and supplier invoices. Record sales, dispatches, customer returns, supplier returns, write-offs and branch transfers that have already happened. A carton standing by the door can otherwise appear as stock in the physical count while its dispatch has already reduced the system quantity.

Separate stock the business owns from stock held for someone else. Consignment, repair, customer deposit, demonstration or supplier-return goods need clear labels. Equally, business-owned goods at another location should not disappear simply because they are off the main premises. Confirm ownership and location from the agreement and movement evidence.

4. Count in a route that prevents gaps and duplication

Plan a fixed route through the premises, such as left to right and top to bottom, then mark every completed bay, shelf, bin, refrigerator or locked cabinet. Do not let counters choose only the areas that look important. Include goods behind displays, under counters, in vehicles and in temporary receiving or returns spaces when they are in scope.

Count sealed packaging only when the packaging quantity is reliable and intact. Open, damaged or mixed cartons need verification. Weighed or measured goods need suitable equipment, a recorded unit and a consistent method. Do not convert a visual estimate into an exact quantity without disclosing that it is an estimate.

For controlled, high-value, regulated, perishable or frequently disputed items, use a second independent count. The second counter should not simply read the first result and agree. Where results differ, perform a supervised recount and preserve all three entries rather than erasing the earlier numbers.

5. Separate quantity from condition

A physically present unit is not automatically saleable stock. Record expired goods, damaged packaging, contamination, breakage, missing parts, obsolete models, customer returns awaiting decision and items reserved for confirmed orders. Keep these quantities visible so they cannot be sold accidentally or counted twice after they move to another area.

Do not destroy or dispose of questionable stock during the count unless safety rules require immediate action. Label and quarantine it, record the reason and follow the applicable business, insurer, regulator, environmental or tax procedure. Photographs can support a condition record, but they do not by themselves prove ownership, quantity, cost or an authorised write-off.

The IFRS Foundation IAS 2 overview says inventories are measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value and explains that inventory cost includes costs incurred to bring goods to their present location and condition. That is an accounting framework, not a licence to improvise valuations. The AfroTools tracker multiplies the unit cost entered by quantity on hand; it does not apply FIFO, weighted average, write-downs or a formal accounting policy.

6. Reconcile the physical count to the movement record

For each item, compare the physical quantity with the book quantity at the same cut-off. A basic quantity check is:

Expected closing quantity = opening quantity + receipts + returns in + transfers in minus sales or issues minus returns out minus transfers out minus approved losses.

Use that relationship as a tracing structure, not as proof that the records are correct. A missing sale, duplicated receipt or wrong unit conversion can make the expected quantity unreliable. Match movements to dated receipts, invoices, delivery notes, sales records, return documents and approved adjustment references.

The Uganda Revenue Authority business-record guidance lists stock records among the records a business may need and says income and expense records should identify items, dates, quantities, units and amounts. It also advises recording transactions immediately or daily to reduce omissions. That supports regular movement recording; it does not make one country's tax procedure universal across Africa.

7. Investigate variances without inventing a reason

Start with the simplest checks: recount the item, verify the SKU and pack unit, search other approved locations, review movements near the cut-off and check whether a receipt, sale, return or transfer was posted twice or not posted at all. Compare similar-looking variants that may have been scanned or recorded under the wrong code.

Then review condition and timing. A damaged item may have been moved without an adjustment. A customer return may be physically present but not accepted into saleable stock. A delivery may have been invoiced but only partly received. A mobile or offline sales record may not yet have synchronised. Preserve the uncertainty if the evidence does not resolve it.

Do not label a staff member, customer or supplier responsible based on the variance alone. Shrinkage can arise from process errors, waste, damage, fraud or several causes together. Any allegation or disciplinary action needs its own fair evidence and applicable procedure. The stocktake provides a signal and an audit trail, not a verdict.

8. Approve adjustments with a traceable record

Keep the original book quantity, physical quantity, variance, investigation note, supporting references, approver and adjustment date. Use reason categories consistently, but leave room for an honest unresolved status. A vague description such as "correction" hides whether the issue was a unit error, late receipt, spoilage, supplier return, customer return or unexplained shortage.

In Kenya, the KRA eTIMS guidance says its stock-management module assists taxpayers in maintaining inventory and links invoicing records with the taxpayer portal. Ghana's E-VAT guidelines require applicable registered persons to keep sales and purchase journals, invoices, receipts, import and export documents, and debit or credit evidence. If an official system applies to the business, reconcile through its current authorised workflow rather than treating a private planning export as a replacement.

Ask the responsible accountant or tax adviser how an approved quantity adjustment affects inventory value, cost of sales, VAT, tax deductions or insurance claims. Physical evidence and accounting entries are connected, but they are not the same decision.

Low stock means quantity on hand is less than or equal to the user-set reorder point. When target stock is provided, suggested reorder units are target stock minus quantity, with a floor of zero. This arithmetic does not consider open purchase orders, reserved stock, lead time, minimum order quantities, supplier credit, expiry, storage space or available cash.

10. Turn the result into controlled next actions

Resolve urgent safety and expiry issues first. Next, correct approved record errors, complete missing movement documents and investigate material unresolved variances. Only then review purchasing. An apparently low item may already be on an open order, while an apparently healthy quantity may include reserved, damaged or slow-moving stock.

Continue with the inventory reorder-point guide when reliable quantities and movement history are available. Use the Cash-Flow Forecast to place deposits and supplier payments in the periods when cash will leave. For a day-end view of sold stock, losses and expenses, use the Market Stall Daily Profit Planner.

Set a review cycle by risk. Fast-moving, high-value, regulated, perishable or frequently mismatched items may need more frequent cycle counts than stable low-risk goods. Review whether the count process itself worked: were locations missed, were units unclear, did movements continue without logs, and did approval take too long? Improve the process before the next count instead of merely changing the final quantities.

11. Keep a compact stocktake evidence pack

  • Scope, location, date, cut-off time and responsible people.
  • Controlled item list with stable names, SKUs, units and locations.
  • Original count sheets, recounts and movement log.
  • Book quantities and dated source reports at the same cut-off.
  • Receipts, invoices, delivery notes, sales, returns and transfer references used in reconciliation.
  • Condition and quarantine record for damaged, expired, held or obsolete stock.
  • Variance investigation, approval and unresolved-item register.
  • Approved adjustment output and protected backup.

Retain records for the period and format required by the relevant authority and business policy. Ghana's E-VAT guidelines specify a six-year minimum for the records covered by that guidance. Uganda's separate business-formalisation guidance describes a five-year period for applicable business records. These country examples show why a single retention period should not be presented as an Africa-wide rule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a stocktake the same as an inventory valuation?

No. A stocktake establishes physical quantities and condition. Accounting valuation requires an approved cost method, supporting records and any required treatment of damaged or obsolete stock.

Should a shop count stock while sales continue?

Avoid uncontrolled movement during the count. If the shop must keep trading, record every receipt, sale, return and transfer during the count window and assign a clear cut-off.

Can a stock variance prove theft?

No. A variance is a control signal. Recheck the count, units, receipts, sales, returns, transfers, damage and data-entry history before deciding why it occurred.

Can the AfroTools inventory tracker replace tax or accounting records?

No. It is a browser-local planning tracker. It does not supply an accounting valuation method, tax filing, official e-invoicing, demand forecast or independent verification.

What should happen after a stocktake?

Approve explained adjustments, preserve the count evidence, update the controlled stock record, investigate unresolved differences and review reorder settings only after quantities are reliable.
