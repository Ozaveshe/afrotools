A cold chain is not a cold room followed by an ordinary delivery. It is a connected temperature-control workflow from harvest, landing or production through pre-cooling, packing, storage, loading, transport, unloading, market storage and final handover. A break at one transfer can erase the value of the equipment used everywhere else.

This guide was verified on August 17, 2026. The source set includes the Food and Agriculture Organization's March 30, 2026 cold-chain update, its active 2025 to 2026 food-loss programme for selected African countries, FAO cold-storage operating guidance, the current UNEP sustainable cold-chain programme page and the World Bank's Africa food-transport report page. It is a planning workflow, not a food-safety approval, engineering design or guaranteed investment return. Product temperature limits, hygiene controls and licensing must come from the current rules and competent authorities for the specific food and market.

1. Map The Complete Cold Chain Before Pricing Equipment

FAO describes the upstream chain as the work immediately after harvest, including pre-cooling, processing, packaging and short-term storage near production. The downstream chain covers transport, distribution, wholesale or market storage, retail display and delivery. That distinction is useful because a warehouse quote often covers only one point in a longer chain.

Draw one line from the product's first temperature-sensitive point to the buyer's acceptance point. Record every location, transfer, expected duration, responsible party and evidence produced. Include waiting at farm gates, landing sites, packhouses, consolidation yards, borders, ports and customer receiving bays. Waiting time is part of the route even when the vehicle is not moving.

For each step, capture five facts:

  • The product condition at entry and the required condition at exit.
  • The time allowed for cooling, holding, loading and travel.
  • The equipment or service that maintains the required condition.
  • The temperature, seal, time or inspection record that proves performance.
  • The party that pays when product is delayed, rejected, damaged or lost.

Do not combine chilled, frozen and ambient-stable products into one generic temperature assumption. Do not copy a set point from another commodity. The product specification, process, packaging, maturity, food-safety plan and destination requirements determine the operating range. The cost model begins only after that requirement is clear.

2. Measure The Existing Loss And Service Baseline

The current FAO Food Coalition programme for selected African countries starts with value-chain diagnostics and food-loss baselines before piloting storage or solar cold rooms. A business should follow the same sequence. If the location and cause of loss are unknown, new cooling equipment can move the bottleneck without improving saleable output.

Use receiving, production, dispatch and customer records to measure kilograms entering, kilograms sold in the agreed condition, kilograms downgraded, kilograms rejected and kilograms discarded. Separate physical loss from quality discount, weight loss, delayed sale and customer claim. Record where each outcome first became visible and where its cause most likely occurred.

Keep value separate from volume. One kilogram discarded does not always carry the same commercial impact as one kilogram downgraded, reworked or sold late. Use the actual selling price or contribution margin supported by invoices and cost records. If those values vary by grade or season, preserve the bands instead of forcing one annual average.

A credible baseline also includes service performance: collection reliability, journey time, border or port dwell, temperature excursions, power outages, equipment downtime, maintenance response and customer rejection. A cold-chain investment should have a named problem to solve and a measure that can show whether it worked.

3. Build A Delivered-Cost Model, Not A Cold-Room Budget

Price the chain by period, route and saleable output. A useful calculation is:

Cold-chain cost per delivered kilogram = total cold-chain cost for the period divided by kilograms accepted at the delivery point.

The denominator matters. Dividing by kilograms purchased or harvested hides the cost of residual loss. For a service that charges by crate, pallet, trip or day, convert the charge to the same delivered-kilogram basis before comparing options.

Annualize capital only over a supportable life and include replacement cycles for components such as batteries, sensors and door seals. Keep residual value as an explicit assumption. For a rental or cooling-as-a-service proposal, include minimum volumes, take-or-pay terms, peak-season rates, escalation, rejected-load rules and termination costs.

Calculate avoided-loss break-even without inventing a market price. Divide the incremental cold-chain cost by the contribution margin retained for each additional accepted kilogram. The result is the minimum additional saleable volume required to cover the incremental cost. If the margin is uncertain, calculate several evidence-backed bands from actual sales records.

4. Price The Cooling Load, Power Source And Failure Plan

FAO's 2026 update notes that cold chains depend heavily on reliable energy and identifies decentralized solar as one possible source for cold rooms, pre-cooling, ice production and refrigerated containers. Solar can be valuable, but the commercial question is not whether panels are cheaper in general. It is whether the complete system can meet the measured cooling load and recovery requirement at the specific site.

Ask an engineer or supplier to separate the pull-down load from the holding load. Product entering warm can require much more cooling than product already at the correct condition. FAO's fish cold-storage guidance warns that a store intended to hold frozen product may not have spare capacity to freeze or rapidly cool warm incoming product. Door openings, ambient heat, air leakage, loading pattern, insulation, defrost cycles and product load all affect energy use.

Obtain a load profile, not only a compressor nameplate. Meter the facility where possible. Price grid energy, solar generation, battery or thermal storage, generator fuel, maintenance and automatic or manual changeover. Record how long the product can remain within its accepted condition during an outage and how quickly the system must recover afterward.

Use the Business Outage Cost tool to organize gross avoidable loss from spoilage, idle labour and recovery. Use the Solar ROI Calculator to compare user-supplied power, maintenance, financing and backup assumptions. Neither tool designs a refrigeration system. Keep engineering capacity, refrigerant choice, electrical protection, fire safety and food controls with qualified providers.

5. Cost The Route And Every Temperature-Control Handoff

The World Bank's current Africa food-transport report page connects food availability with roads, ports, border crossings, storage and distribution. For a cold chain, delay is both a logistics cost and a temperature-control exposure. Route planning should therefore include elapsed time, waiting time and the power or thermal strategy while the load is stationary.

Quote the whole movement: empty positioning, collection, pre-trip inspection, loading, seals, route, tolls, borders, driver time, reefer fuel or power, unloading, cleaning and the return leg. State whether the carrier supplies temperature data, who reviews alarms and how long records are retained. A cheaper trip without usable evidence can become expensive when a buyer disputes product condition.

At each handoff, record arrival time, departure time, vehicle or container identity, seal status, product condition, temperature evidence and receiving acceptance. Agree the response to a delay or excursion before dispatch. The plan may involve moving the load, using alternate storage, adding approved cooling media, notifying the buyer or stopping delivery. It should not be invented during a failure.

Utilization shapes the rate. A partly loaded refrigerated vehicle can have a high cost per delivered kilogram even when the trip price looks competitive. Consolidation can improve economics only when products are compatible, schedules align and loading does not block airflow or compromise separation. Use the truck-load and route-cost tools to compare user-entered capacity and route inputs, then confirm the operating plan with the carrier.

6. Compare Ownership, Rental And Cooling-As-A-Service

FAO's cold-storage guidance notes that construction and operating costs vary with local materials, labour and electricity, and that containerized stores may be bought or rented. It also ties return on investment to the market value of the product and use of the facility. A high-capacity store is not automatically the better investment if it remains underused for much of the year.

Compare options at the same usable volume, product condition, service hours, monitoring standard, maintenance response and backup requirement:

  • Ownership gives more control but places design, utilization, maintenance, energy and replacement risk on the business.
  • Rental can reduce initial capital, but the contract must define capacity, access, power, monitoring, liability and escalation.
  • Cooling as a service may convert part of the cost to a usage fee, but minimum volume and availability commitments still matter.
  • Third-party refrigerated logistics can cover storage and movement, but service evidence and claims rules must be explicit.

Stress-test low utilization, peak harvest volume, longer route time, higher energy cost, equipment downtime, delayed customer payment and a lower saleable price. Do not add the worst value for every assumption and call it a forecast. Keep a base case supported by current records, then show named downside conditions separately.

7. Pilot The Smallest Complete Chain And Keep The Evidence

A useful pilot covers the complete route for a defined product and customer, not only a successful cold-room test. Set the starting batch, accepted condition, route, responsibilities, monitoring points and commercial success measure before the product enters the chain. Preserve the same records for the current process so the comparison has a baseline.

During the pilot, track product volume and grade, cooling start and finish, room and product readings, door openings where available, energy consumption, outages, transport times, handoff records, accepted kilograms, discounts, rejections and direct costs. Investigate missing data rather than treating it as a pass.

FAO guidance calls for proper maintenance, airflow, continuous temperature recording and prompt action when conditions move outside tolerated limits. Translate those controls into a daily operating checklist. Assign responsibility for sensor checks, alarm response, defrost and drainage, door discipline, cleaning, stock rotation, maintenance calls and customer evidence.

At the end of the pilot, recalculate cost per accepted kilogram and avoided-loss break-even from actual records. Check whether the gain came from lower loss, a higher accepted grade, faster sale, better market access or a combination. Scale only the part that was demonstrated. A useful cold chain is one that protects the product, produces usable evidence and improves the economics of a real route.

Frequently Asked Questions

What costs belong in an African cold-chain budget?

Include pre-cooling, handling, storage, electricity, backup power, maintenance, monitoring, labour, packaging, refrigerated transport, waiting time, finance and measured residual spoilage or rejection. Keep product purchase cost separate so the cold-chain decision remains visible.

Should a food business buy a cold room or pay for cold storage?

Compare both options at the same usable capacity, service level, temperature requirement, expected utilization and risk allocation. Low or seasonal utilization can make a service contract more resilient than an underused owned facility, while stable high use can strengthen the case for ownership.

How do you calculate cold-chain cost per kilogram?

Add the period's operating cost and the period share of capital or service fees, then divide by kilograms accepted at the delivery point. Use actual accepted output rather than kilograms purchased if part of the load was lost, downgraded or rejected.

Can solar power remove the need for cold-room backup?

No single power design suits every site. Model the actual cooling load, solar resource, storage or thermal capacity, grid reliability, outage duration and recovery requirement before choosing solar, batteries, generators or a hybrid system.
