Inflation Rates Across Africa 2026: Sources and Planning Guide

By Idris Diallo Published Mar 19, 2026 8 min read
Idris Diallo Mar 19, 2026 Sources and workflow reviewed: October 1, 2026 8 min read

To compare inflation rates across Africa in 2026, start with the period and measure. A national CPI release for one month and an IMF forecast for a whole year can both be useful, but they cannot be read as the same statistic. This guide gives a dated comparison for five economies, links to official CPI publishers, and shows how to turn a sourced rate into a purchasing-power calculation.

Review scope: Sources and calculator behavior were checked on October 1, 2026. The comparison below uses the April 2026 IMF release and covers five economies. For current monthly figures, use the dated national releases linked below. Calculations here are planning estimates, not investment advice or official salary recommendations.

Where to find official CPI releases

Open the publisher's CPI or consumer-prices release and check its reference period. A page's update date alone does not tell you which month a figure measures. Save the release title or URL beside your rate so another person can reproduce the comparison.

  • Nigeria: National Bureau of Statistics. Check headline and food inflation separately, and retain any rebasing or methodology notes.
  • Kenya: Kenya National Bureau of Statistics CPI releases. Locate the CPI and inflation release, then distinguish the monthly change from the annual change.
  • Ghana: Ghana Statistical Service. Read the CPI bulletin and its coverage before using a headline figure for a food-heavy household budget.
  • South Africa: Statistics South Africa CPI releases, P0141. Use the release's reference month and the relevant expenditure category.
  • Egypt: Central Bank of Egypt inflation statistics. Keep urban headline, nationwide and core measures distinct when comparing releases.

If a release is unavailable, label the older observation with its actual period. Do not substitute a forecast and present it as today's rate. If a country has multiple currency-based measures, record the currency and series definition as well.

Four inflation measures that answer different questions

The World Bank's CPI inflation metadata describes annual inflation in terms of the changing cost of a representative consumer basket. Your household's spending mix can differ from that basket.

  1. Month-on-month: compares a price index with the preceding month. It describes a short interval, rather than the change over a full year.
  2. Year-on-year: compares a month with the same month a year earlier. Retain the month when quoting it.
  3. Annual average: compares average price levels across years. This is the measure used in the IMF table above.
  4. End-of-period: describes the change at the end of the stated period. Do not take a value from that column and label it an annual average.

The IMF WEO data FAQ explains how estimates and projections are identified. The WEO database contains annual series rather than monthly CPI readings. Its figures can differ from national publications because of timing, revisions and methodology.

Before comparing two countries, check all four items: measure, period, geography and publication vintage. Also distinguish an inflation rate from an index level. A CPI level of 120 in one country's series and 150 in another does not, by itself, establish which country is more expensive to live in.

Calculate price equivalents and purchasing power

Use the AfroTools Inflation Scenario Calculator with a rate you have sourced. Enter the starting amount, annual rate, period in years, source label and date you checked it. The worksheet holds that rate constant; it does not predict future inflation.

If A is the starting amount, r is the annual rate as a decimal, and t is the number of years:

  • Amount needed later for the same basket: A × (1 + r)t.
  • Purchasing power of unchanged money: A ÷ (1 + r)t.
  • Percentage loss in purchasing power: [1 − 1 ÷ (1 + r)t] × 100.

A percentage increase and the resulting purchasing-power loss are not equal. The price increase multiplies the basket's cost, while the purchasing-power calculation divides by the same factor. Use the formula rather than subtracting the inflation percentage from your balance.

For an observed period with changing inflation, use matched CPI index levels: multiply the earlier amount by the later index divided by the earlier index. Do not repeatedly apply today's year-on-year rate to reconstruct history. Keep both index observations from the same compatible series.

Use inflation in a salary or budget review

Choose a baseline date and compare your recurring spending at that date with the same categories now. Separate food, housing, utilities, transport, education and other essentials. Keep quantities comparable: a larger grocery purchase is not automatically a price increase.

Headline CPI provides a reference point for the conversation. Your receipts show how your own basket changed. A salary request can cite both, with their dates and limits, without claiming that CPI requires an employer to award a particular increase. Compare take-home pay when taxes or deductions have also changed; use the relevant salary and tax tools for that separate calculation.

For a business, use actual supplier quotes and input costs alongside CPI. A household consumer basket does not necessarily track imported equipment, wholesale stock or commercial rent. Record a review date and avoid treating a broad national rate as proof of an exact price adjustment.

Compare savings returns with inflation

First establish the return after the fees and taxes that apply, over the same period as your inflation assumption. For a nominal return g and inflation r, the exact real return is [(1 + g) ÷ (1 + r)] − 1. Express both inputs as decimals. Simply subtracting the two percentages is an approximation.

This calculation does not settle whether an account or investment suits your needs. Check access to the money, capital risk, deposit protection where applicable, charges and currency exposure. An advertised rate is not proof of a guaranteed return above inflation. Money needed for near-term bills also has a different purpose from long-term savings.

If you receive diaspora remittances, compare the local amount delivered after transfer charges with the cost of the intended purchases. More local-currency units do not automatically buy more goods. Keep exchange-rate movement and local price movement as separate calculations.
