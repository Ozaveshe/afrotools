Grain Storage Loss Planning for African Farms

AfroTools TeamAug 11, 202614 min read

A grain storage plan should show more than how many bags entered a store. It should record how much clean, saleable grain entered, the condition of that grain, what left the store for food or sale, what remained, what became unfit or downgraded, and the cost of protecting the stock. Without that chain of evidence, a loss percentage can mix insect damage, moisture change, household withdrawals, spillage, rejected grain and ordinary sales into one unreliable number.

Use the AfroTools Grain Storage Loss Calculator to compare a current method with hermetic bags or a metal silo, but replace general defaults with farm records and current local quotes. This guide's primary sources were checked on August 11, 2026. They include the African Postharvest Losses Information System, its cereal loss assessment manual, FAO storage guidance, Purdue University's PICS programme and farmer instructions, and CIMMYT's Kenya hermetic-storage research summary.

Planning boundary: APHLIS estimates describe large areas and value-chain stages. They are not a measurement of one farmer's store. The calculator is a planning tool, not a guarantee of weight saved, food safety, future price or investment return.

Define what counts as storage loss

APHLIS treats postharvest loss as a reduction in the weight of grain ready for human consumption across harvesting, farm storage, transport and market storage. Its system separates cumulative percentage loss, the absolute weight lost and loss density by area. It also explains that quality change matters to the weight-loss estimate only when food is no longer fit for human consumption, even though a quality downgrade can still reduce the farmer's selling price.

That distinction matters in a farm record. Grain may lose sale value without disappearing. Broken kernels, insect holes, discoloration, odour, mould, contamination or a buyer's moisture rejection can produce a discount. Record that as a quality or price loss. Grain consumed by the household, retained as seed, fed deliberately to animals or sold should be recorded as an authorized stock movement, not quietly counted as storage loss. Spillage, pest consumption and grain discarded because it is unfit belong in the loss record.

Use APHLIS as context, then measure the store

APHLIS combines published research with observations from local experts to estimate postharvest losses at a scale that would be expensive to observe directly. The platform lets users compare crops, countries, regions, years and value-chain stages. It can also map factors such as rain at harvest, climate and the presence of larger grain borer. That makes it useful for identifying risk and testing whether a farm assumption is plausible.

The APHLIS guidance is equally clear about the limit. A provincial estimate is a generalisation. Actual losses vary by locality, season, crop, farm scale and storage practice. The platform recommends using its downloadable calculator and changing defaults when a user has recent local evidence. For an individual store, record weights and stock movements rather than copying a continental or national percentage into the business plan.

The APHLIS loss assessment manual also distinguishes relative loss from absolute loss. Its worked explanation uses 17.5 percent of 1,000 tonnes, which equals 175 tonnes lost and 825 tonnes left. The arithmetic is simple, but the evidence behind the percentage is the hard part. On a farm, calculate both forms:

  • Absolute storage loss: clean opening stock minus authorized withdrawals minus clean closing stock.
  • Relative storage loss: absolute storage loss divided by the stock exposed to storage risk, multiplied by 100.
  • Direct value loss: saleable weight lost multiplied by the verified price for the relevant grade and sale date.
  • Quality discount: expected value at the supported grade minus realized value after a documented downgrade.

Keep moisture measurement in the same record because a weight change can reflect water movement as well as dry matter loss. If opening and closing grain were measured at different moisture conditions, ask an extension or postharvest specialist how to compare them on a consistent basis. Do not present all weight change as grain eaten by pests.

Inspect drying before choosing a container

FAO storage guidance identifies moisture content as one of the critical factors in successful grain storage. The safe limit depends on the grain, storage conditions and intended duration. Grain above the suitable limit is more vulnerable to fungi, insects and rapid deterioration. Temperature, relative humidity and moisture interact, so a dry-looking surface is not enough evidence for a long storage decision.

Use a moisture meter or the crop-specific method recommended by a competent local extension service, warehouse, buyer or standards authority. Record the instrument, sampling points, date and result. Sample more than the top of a large batch because moisture can be uneven. Remove foreign material and visibly damaged grain before sealing. If rain or high humidity affects the batch after measurement, inspect and test again.

A hermetic container does not repair poor drying. Airtight storage is designed to restrict gas exchange and control storage insects. It can also restrict later moisture movement, which means grain should enter in the right condition. Do not use one internet moisture percentage as a universal rule for maize, rice, sorghum, millet, cowpea, beans and groundnuts. Confirm the current crop-specific threshold and buyer requirement for the location.

Compare the full storage systems

Traditional bags, improved woven bags with a hermetic liner, triple-layer PICS bags, rigid plastic systems and metal silos have different costs and controls. The choice is not only bag price. It includes drying, cleaning, handling, sealing, store condition, pest pressure, expected duration, inspection, repair, financing and the value of stock placed at risk.

CIMMYT and the Kenya Agricultural Research Institute tested hermetic systems for maize storage at three Kenyan sites. Their summary concluded that hermetic systems could control storage insects without insecticides, while also identifying practical questions about metal-silo cost, bag perforation and the economics of reuse. That is the right investment mindset. Technical control can work while a specific product or store size still fails the farm's cost test.

Follow the hermetic bag operating controls

Purdue's PICS farmer poster begins with dry, clean grain and approved bags. It instructs users to inspect the two inner liners for holes or tears, place them correctly inside the outer woven bag, fill while limiting air pockets, and tie each layer securely. It also recommends keeping bags out of direct sunlight and extreme heat, using an elevated platform, leaving space from walls for inspection and avoiding heavily infested grain in the same area.

Turn those instructions into a check sheet for every bag:

  1. Record crop, variety where known, owner, lot, clean weight, moisture result and filling date.
  2. Inspect the outer bag and every inner liner under good light. Reject or repair only as the manufacturer permits.
  3. Remove sharp debris and prepare a clean filling area that will not puncture the liners.
  4. Fill and close each layer according to the current manufacturer or extension instructions.
  5. Place the sealed unit on a dry elevated surface, away from walls, sunlight, water and sharp equipment.
  6. Inspect the store on a schedule for rodents, water entry, disturbed bags, holes, failed closures and theft.
  7. At opening, record clean output weight, damaged or discarded weight, grade, buyer result and the condition of each bag.

Purdue reports that PICS technology has been commercialized widely through licensed manufacturers and distributors, with farmer training across many countries. Scale does not remove the need to verify the actual product. Buy from an approved or trusted channel, confirm the bag type and instructions, and reject a product with damaged liners or uncertain specification. Do not assume that every bag marketed as hermetic has the same construction, testing or service life.

Calculate return from value actually protected

The calculator can compare methods, but the return calculation should start with the same crop, weight, duration and value basis. Do not compare a three-month traditional-bag record with a nine-month hermetic forecast and call the difference proven. Keep the exposure period and stock movements consistent.

Use this structure:

  • Value protected: the difference in saleable grain value between the improved and current methods, supported by measured weight and realized grade.
  • Net storage benefit: value protected plus any documented price improvement, minus extra storage-system, drying, testing, handling, repair, finance and selling costs.
  • Return on storage investment: net storage benefit divided by the incremental cost of the improved method, multiplied by 100.
  • Payback batches: incremental storage-system cost divided by measured net benefit per comparable batch.

A seasonal price increase should be recorded only when supported by actual sales or a dated buyer quotation for the same grade and location. Waiting can also create finance, security and opportunity costs. If the farmer needs cash immediately after harvest, the ability to hold grain may be limited even when later prices are higher. Separate the benefit of reducing physical loss from the benefit or risk of delaying sale.

Run a controlled comparison before expanding

When practical, divide one clean, well-mixed lot into clearly weighed batches and store them for the same period under the current and proposed methods. Record entry condition, container, location, inspections, withdrawals and closing results. This does not replace laboratory or extension guidance, but it produces farm-specific evidence that is more useful than a copied average.

Review more than closing weight. Compare saleable weight, discarded weight, buyer grade, realized price, moisture, insect damage, labour, packaging failures and cash tied up. If one batch becomes visibly mouldy, has an unusual odour, is contaminated or raises a food-safety concern, isolate it and seek competent local advice. A calculator must never be used to declare questionable grain safe.

Scale only after the record answers four questions: Did the proposed method protect more saleable value? Did the store team follow the method correctly? Did the full cost remain affordable? Can the same bag, silo and store controls be maintained for a larger stock? If the answer to any question is uncertain, improve the next controlled batch before buying a full-season capacity.

Build the storage file for the next season

Keep one storage file per crop and season. Include supplier quotes, purchase receipts, moisture records, opening weights, bag or silo IDs, inspection logs, pest observations, authorized withdrawals, closing weights, quality results, sales receipts and photos that do not expose private personal data. Review the file with the Farm Profit Calculator so storage loss and storage cost appear in the full enterprise result, then use the Crop Yield Estimator only as a planning input for expected harvest volume.

The useful outcome is not the lowest loss percentage displayed on a screen. It is a repeatable storage decision that can be checked from harvest to sale, separates physical and quality loss, protects food safety, and shows whether the value preserved exceeded the real cost of the method.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can one percentage describe grain storage loss across Africa?

No. Crop, location, harvest conditions, drying, storage duration, pests, store design, handling and stock withdrawals all affect loss. Use APHLIS estimates as contextual evidence and replace defaults with measured local records where possible.

What should be checked before grain enters a hermetic bag?

Confirm that the grain is dry and clean for the crop and local extension standard, inspect every liner for holes, keep the bag away from sharp surfaces, and prepare an elevated, dry storage area where bags can be inspected.

How is the value protected by improved storage calculated?

Compare saleable grain and realized value under the current method with the improved method, then subtract the full cost of bags or silos, handling, drying, testing, repairs, finance and any extra storage period.

Does the AfroTools calculator guarantee a storage return?

No. It is a planning estimate. Actual weight, quality, price, moisture, pests, bag integrity, storage duration and buyer acceptance must be measured and confirmed locally.
