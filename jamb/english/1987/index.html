Question 1

There are one or two things this country can teach others, one of which is the art of writing obituaries. One suspects that the reason why some of our newspapers still manage to break even is because of the great amount of revenue they derive from obituaries. It is not unusual for about one quarter of the volume of an average daily to be constituted of obituaries and in memoriam alone. One possible explanation for this, it has been argued, is that Nigerians value their dead greatly. And there is a saying amongst us that you do not say evil things against the dead. This is obviously the philosophy behind the large dose of encomiums with which our dead are bestowed. From the evidences of these obituaries and In memoriam, every dead Nigerian must have been something of a saint while alive. This would explain why the death of most Nigerians is attributed to the evil machinations of the wicked. Only very few people in our country die natural death, and even when they do, the obituaries etc. always give the impression that such deaths constitute the saddest loss to befall the deceased’s family. And that is why writers of these obituaries and their allied advertisements are experts on ‘mortuary stylistics’. This ‘mortuary stylistics’, the study in the art of eulogizing the dead and making their loss sound so heart breaking, is one of the commodities we can export to other countries.

The tone of this passage is

  1. angry
  2. satirical
  3. non-commital
  4. pleasant.
Answer and explanation

B: satirical

The writer mocks exaggerated praise and the suggestion that obituary writing could be exported. This ironic treatment of a social practice gives the passage a satirical tone rather than a neutral or angry one.

The dominant subject of this passage is the

  1. white man’s road
  2. new religion

C: white man’s influence

The speaker links roads, soldiers and religion as parts of the same colonial presence. The white man’s influence therefore covers the passage as a whole, while each other option covers only one part.

Question 16

Primitive man was probably more concerned with fire as a source of warmth and as a means of cooking food than as a source of light. Before he discovered less laborious ways of making fire, he had to preserve it and whenever he went on a journey, he carried a firebrand with him. Lamps too probably developed by accident. Early man may have had his first conception of a lamp while watching a twig or fiber burning in the molten fat dropped from roasting carcass. All he had to do was to fashion a vessel to contain fat and float a lighted reed in it. Such lamps which were made of hollow stones or sea-shells have persisted in identical form up to quite recent times.

Primitive man carried a firebrand during his journeys mainly for

  1. illumination
  2. cooking of food
  3. flame preservation
  4. warmth.
Answer and explanation

C: flame preservation

The passage says the firebrand was carried primarily to preserve a flame before easy methods of making fire existed. Its usefulness for light was a later or incidental discovery.

Question 21

Developments in electronic science have transformed the art of record keeping in the modern age. Traditionally, records of events were kept only in people’s minds. With the advent of the electronic memory, however, these dangers have been largely overcome. The author believes that electronic recording is

  1. superior only to mental recording
  2. inferior to both mental recording and writing
  3. superior to both mental recording and writing
  4. inferior to only writing.
Answer and explanation

C: superior to both mental recording and writing

The writer describes mental records and writing as vulnerable to forgetting or falsehood, then presents electronic recording as overcoming much of that risk. The claimed superiority is over both earlier methods.
